Search Intelligence Capstone · CTR / Engagement Opportunity Scoring

The simple rule beat the trained model.

A position-tier lookup table outranked a Random Forest on 176,568 real pages from FlyRank's search warehouse — and figuring out why turned out to be the actual finding.

00 — Abstract

Which already-ranking pages under-earn clicks for their position, and what should a content team fix first? Using FlyRank's 79-million-row search warehouse, I built a position-tier CTR baseline and compared it against a Random Forest trained on position, content, and keyword features, validated with a client-grouped split to prevent leakage. The Random Forest showed an 8.8% lower prediction error than the baseline — but a weaker rank correlation (0.057 vs. 0.166), which matters more here since the actual deliverable is a ranked list, not a point estimate. The honest conclusion: on this data, added features do not yet outrank the simple lookup table. The baseline logic was carried forward into a five-archetype action playbook (72,816 actionable pages), with every threshold and limitation checked against real query output rather than assumed.

01 — Introduction

The decision this supports

Content that ranks well in search doesn't automatically get clicked. A page sitting in position 3 can still under-perform its peers — a weak title, a mismatched meta description, or thin content can all suppress click-through rate (CTR) even when ranking is otherwise healthy. For a content team managing thousands of pages, the question isn't "is CTR a problem somewhere" — it's "which specific pages, out of thousands, are worth an editor's time this week?"

That's the decision this project supports: a content or SEO lead deciding where to spend limited editing hours, backed by a ranked, reasoned list rather than a gut-feel scan of a spreadsheet.

Why this isn't just "train a model" The hard part was never fitting a regressor — it was building an honest baseline to beat, validating without leaking client identity across train/test, and translating a score into a recommendation a human could act on and refuse to automate blindly. Each of those steps changed the final answer.

02 — Data

What went in, on purpose — and what didn't

All analysis uses FlyRank's internship-warehouse release on Hugging Face: fact_content_daily_performance (78.8M rows, report_date × client × content) joined to dim_content (519,606 rows, one per content item), filtered to March 2026 — a mid-panel month, not the sealed final month reserved as an untouched test period.

Unit of analysis

One row = one content item belonging to one client, with organic search performance summed over March 2026. Grain was verified, not assumed: a GROUP BY report_date, client, content HAVING COUNT(*) > 1 probe returned zero violating rows before any feature was built.

Deliberately excluded

  • Raw query text and the query-level table (fact_content_query_90d) — its 90-day rolling window doesn't align cleanly with a single calendar month and risks the label period leaking into the feature period.
  • Client and content identifiers — used only as hashed pseudonyms for grouping and joining, never as model features, never resolved back to a real name, brand, or URL.
  • content_updated_date as a "days since update" feature — a check during data-contract work found 84% of March rows had this field dated after March, meaning it reflects a current snapshot timestamp, not month-specific history. Using it would have been an accidental future-information leak.
A limitation worth stating plainly 58 of 104 clients — the majority — have no usable March GSC data and are invisible to every result in this paper. Nothing here describes those clients, and no finding should be assumed to transfer to them.

03 — Methodology

Baseline first, model second, leakage hunted before either was trusted

The baseline: a benchmark you can read in one sentence

CTR is well known to fall off with search position. Rather than assume that shape, I measured it directly on this data — a page's benchmark CTR is the mean observed CTR of all pages sharing its position tier.

A second candidate signal — content age — was tested the same way and did not hold up: CTR by age tier moved 0.38% → 0.29% → 0.63% → 0.30%, with the 180–365 day tier posting nearly double the CTR of the newest content. That's mixed, not confirmed — age alone was excluded as a standalone rule input.

The leakage trap

Before trusting any model, I deliberately added one label-derived column to a toy regression and watched what happened:

Deliberate leakage test

The "leaky" feature was CTR itself under a different column name. A model handed both the question and the answer scores perfectly by lookup, not by learning anything — the textbook shape of a leakage bug, reproduced deliberately here so it could be recognized and ruled out everywhere else in this project.

The model, and how it was validated

A Random Forest Regressor was trained on five features — avg_position, word_count, search_volume, competition, age_days — plus one-hot encoded content_type, predicting observed CTR. Validation used 5-fold GroupKFold grouped by client, not a random split: pages from the same client can share hidden site-level traits, and a random split would let the model partly memorize a client's baseline CTR rather than learn transferable feature relationships. Zero client overlap between train and test was confirmed in every fold before any metric was trusted.

A before/after check compared this grouped split against a naive random split. The result was genuinely mixed — random split showed a worse MAE (0.00864) but a better R² (0.013 vs. −0.028) than the grouped split, stable across two random seeds. Higher R² under random splitting is the direction a leakage effect would produce, but MAE and rank correlation moved the opposite way — an ambiguous result, reported as such rather than forced into a tidier story.

The model won on error. The baseline won on rank — and rank is what matters here.

All metrics computed out-of-fold on the client-grouped split. Random Forest: 8.8% lower MAE than baseline; also the weakest ranking correlation of the three.

Why the ranking metric outweighs the error metric here This lane's actual output is a ranked action list, not a point prediction — what a content team needs is the order right, not the exact decimal. By Spearman correlation, the plain lookup table ranks pages more sensibly than either learned model. Given "don't reward complexity alone," the honest read is that neither model is a clear upgrade over the baseline for this task's real purpose, at this feature set and grain.

Where the model is most wrong

Error was investigated, not just reported. Prediction error climbs sharply at low impression counts: rows with ≤3 impressions carry a mean absolute error of 0.0239, 4.5× the 0.0053 seen above that floor. Every one of the ten single-worst predictions shares one trait — exactly one impression, one click, a "perfect" 100% CTR the model correctly refused to trust. That's the model behaving sensibly around noise, not failing.

05 — Limitations

What this work cannot claim

  • No causal claim, anywhere. Every recommendation here is a correlational, decision-support suggestion. Nothing in this paper claims a recommended change will move ranking or traffic, and nothing claims insight into how Google's ranking algorithm works.
  • Single month, single snapshot. All figures come from March 2026 only. A page flagged this month could look different next month from ordinary variance alone.
  • Majority of clients unrepresented. 58 of 104 clients (56%) have no usable March data and are absent from every number in this paper.
  • A small, quantified survivorship gap. Filtering to currently-published, non-deleted content excludes 0.1% of March-active rows (3,770 of 3.61M) that were live in March but later removed or unpublished — real, but too small to distort the results above.
  • Two of the FlyRank research paper's own findings deserve the same scrutiny applied here. Its content-age "lifecycle" curve compares different pages of different ages at one point in time (cross-sectional), not the same pages tracked over time — a cohort effect is an equally plausible explanation. Its refresh-multiplier finding (3.2× health boost) compares refreshed to un-refreshed pages, but refresh assignment is a choice, not random — part of that lift may reflect which pages get chosen, not the refresh itself.

Opportunity score = (benchmark CTR − observed CTR) × impressions — ranks by click volume at stake, not gap size alone. Full 72,816-row queue ships as a notebook export, not reproduced here.

What should never be automated Auto-publishing a rewrite, auto-deleting or merging pages, or treating opportunity_score itself as a KPI target. A human reviews query intent, SERP layout, and cross-page cannibalization before any change — one client alone had 10,073 pages flagged simultaneously in a single position tier, a scale where a client-level review beats 10,073 individual edits.

Monitoring & retrain triggers

Rebuild monthly. Flag a manual review if any position-tier benchmark drifts more than 20% month-over-month (concrete 2026-03 bounds: top_3 0.0099–0.0149, page_1 0.0039–0.0059, striking 0.0026–0.0039, page_3_5 0.0018–0.0027, deep 0.0007–0.0011), if the low-data archetype share jumps sharply (signals a tracking issue, not a real shift), or if client coverage changes meaningfully from the current 46-of-104.
